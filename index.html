What I do

Entitlement and feasibility, in plain English.

Entitlement is the permission part of real estate: what zoning lets you build, what the city will make you pay for or install, and what it takes to get a yes. I answer those questions before you spend money on the wrong land.

Question

What can you do with your property?

Zoning, setbacks, lot coverage, height, and the unusual things the code quietly allows.

Question

Can you subdivide?

Lot splits and subdivisions: how many lots or units the land can support, and what stands in the way.

Question

What about roads?

Access, frontage, widths, and who pays when a street has to be built or improved.

Question

And sidewalks?

Curb, gutter, and sidewalk requirements that show up late and cost real money if you miss them.

The path from idea to approval: who you meet, what they review, and how long it usually takes.

ADUs

How big can your ADU be? It depends on the city and the lot.

Every city and county sets its own size limit for accessory dwelling units, and the limit is only one piece. Setbacks, lot size, utilities, deed restrictions and new state law all change the answer for your specific property. Idaho is also changing its ADU rules, so what was true last year may not be true next year.

That is the work I do: find out what is allowed on your lot, then put it in writing.

Real deal · Canyon County

How high nitrate levels killed a 53-acre deal.

A 53-acre parcel was on track to be approved for two-acre parcels. Then the nitrate numbers came in. Where groundwater nitrate is high, the county can require bigger lots so there are fewer septic systems per acre, and Canyon County would only approve five-acre lots. That change in lot size killed the deal.

The lesson: lot size is not just a zoning question. On land with septic and well, ask the county and the health district about nitrate limits before you go under contract, and price the deal at the lot size they will actually approve. That check is part of every feasibility study I do.

Speaking the language

Land is counted differently depending on what you build.

The same acre gets measured three ways. Knowing which one a buyer or lender cares about is half of feasibility.

Houses

Lots

For single-family homes, you count how many lots the land will yield once you subtract the space for streets, cul-de-sacs, sidewalks and utility corridors (roads can take 15-30% of the acreage), common areas like parks and paths, and open space, buffers and easements. Steep slopes, drainage areas and flood zones reduce what is buildable, and whether water, sewer, power and stormwater are available matters too, since septic often needs larger lots than city sewer.

Apartments

Doors

For multifamily, the number is units, called doors: how many the zoning and site can support. The density a city allows often depends on local transportation needs and the public transit system, or the lack of one. Parking is another big factor. Codes usually set it as stalls per unit, and one city may require 1.5 stalls per unit while the city across the street requires 1.

Commercial

Property Type

Commercial land will likely have an office, retail or industrial building (or buildings) built on it, based on the local comprehensive plan and demand for that product type. Most cities and counties set a different parking requirement per 1,000 sq ft of rentable building for each type. Parking has a huge impact on how many square feet of office, retail or industrial building can be built.

Commercial land

On commercial, parking sets the size.

Zoning rarely puts a square-foot cap on a commercial building. The limit is usually how much parking fits. Cities like Boise and Meridian commonly require about 4 stalls per 1,000 sq ft of office, and once you fit the stalls, the drive aisles and the building on an acre, it works out to roughly 10,000 sq ft of office per acre.

Why it matters: say you own 1.5 acres of agricultural land next to commercial ground the city just annexed and rezoned. Cities tend to keep annexing the next parcel over, so you are often a strong candidate. I do the homework, then tell you what a 1.5 acre site could realistically hold.

Rule of thumb only. Actual parking ratios, landscaping, and drainage vary by city and by use.
